# How it listens, and its limits

At every note onset the listener compares the sound just after with the sound just before, so strings that are still ringing do not confuse it, and checks which note's harmonics explain what appeared. The app always tells it which notes it is waiting for, and gives those the benefit of the doubt.

**What it has been tested with.** Besides synthesized instruments and the synthesized voice of the practice instrument, the listener is tested against recordings of real instruments and real singers. They are not part of the repository, because most of their licences do not allow passing them on: `npm run samples` fetches them from where their makers published them (see [CREDITS.md](../CREDITS.md)).

- a classical guitar with nylon strings and a grand piano, note by note, soft, normal and loud (University of Iowa Musical Instrument Samples);
- three steel-string electric guitars, recorded directly: plain notes, notes played with the hand lying on the strings, and dead notes, where a string is touched but not pressed down (IDMT-SMT-Guitar, Fraunhofer IDMT; licence CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, so these stay on your computer);
- six pieces strummed on an acoustic guitar into a microphone in a room, with the chords written down beat by beat by the people who recorded them (the same collection, data set 4);
- whole chords on real pianos, as others recorded them: 100 chords played by hand anywhere on the keyboard (the piano part of the chord collection of the University of Liège, published without a licence, so for your own use); 102 triads and sevenths of an upright piano in a room, the microphones at a distance and the keys moved by the instrument itself (MAPS, Télécom Paris; licence CC BY-NC-SA 2.0); and from Wikimedia Commons six chords on a grand piano, C major on an out-of-tune piano recorded at home, and six chords played one after the other, all by hand.
- solo singing: 40 excerpts by 29 singers in seven languages, amateurs and trained, with the pitch written down every 6 ms and the notes by annotators (Vocadito, Bittner and others, 2021, CC BY 4.0; `npm run samples -- voice`).

Songs, patterns and chord changes are put together from the single notes, over room hiss, rumble and a constant offset as a microphone delivers them. On these recordings it

- names 71 of 71 nylon-string notes played normally, 17 of 17 loud and 24 of 25 very soft ones, 107 of 108 steel-string notes and 49 of 49 piano keys;
- follows the picking pattern of the song through nine bars without a mistake at full speed and slower, and finger patterns a semitone at a time on every string (all 72 notes at three notes a second, 70 of 72 at five);
- tells the chords of all 110 changes between the eleven chords apart, hearing each strum once;
- in the six pieces played in a room (G Em C D, C G, Am F E, G C D, Em D; pop, country, latin and reggae strumming, slow and fast) names the chord of 178 of 188 strums it is sure about, and 81 of the 82 chords at least once while they last. The ten misses are single strums in the middle of a chord, nearly all of them taken for the chord before or a neighbour that shares two notes with it (G for Em, G or D for C);
- in the string check calls 72 of 72 clean nylon-string notes and 108 of 108 clean steel-string notes clean, none buzzing; and all 108 dead notes muted, none clean and none "the wrong string";
- names a strum about a fifth of a second after it was played, because it needs that much of the sound. Both games wait for that report before they give a strum up: a stone in Stepping stones is missed only 0.36 s after its window has closed, and a piece of noise rests 0.3 s at the lamp before it counts as having struck it.
- hears two hits across dead strings as the gesture for a pause in 35 of 36 tries, never as a chord; a ringing note in the first five frets is never taken for such a hit. Two muffed notes with more playing after them do not pause the run, however soon the next note comes (96 of 96 tries, the next note 0.15 to 0.45 s later), nor does a note between two muffed ones. Only notes cut so short that they do not count as notes at all (a tenth of a second), followed by a rest, are taken for the gesture now and then;
- reads the open strings steadily enough to tune by (the recorded nylon-string guitar is 15 to 40 cents flat, and it says so), and takes every plucked open string of the nylon-string and the three steel-string guitars for the right string, whichever string the tuner had on show before;
- follows piano melodies and five-finger patterns in both hands, joined up and detached, and measures the piano's A to within a hertz;
- tells chords struck on the piano apart. The keys of the recorded grand are mixed into chords, a few milliseconds apart as a hand strikes them: of 290 changes within six families of chords (the chords of C, of G and of D, sevenths, chords with a fourth, a second or a ninth, and chords on black keys) all 290 are taken for the chord they go to, and every one is heard. The same chord struck four times is heard four times, from half a second down to three tenths of a second apart; a chord broken into keys is heard key by key;
- keeps that up under what a living room and an old piano do to the sound. The same 290 changes were put through a simulated living room (reverberation of half a second and of 0.8 s), with every key up to 10, 20 and 30 cents out of tune with the others, with the whole piano a third of a semitone flat (and the app told so, as at the start of every piano session), with fingers of unequal strength, with keys going down up to 30 ms apart, played a quarter as loud, with the pedal held so that the chord before rings on, and with most of these at once: 290 of 290 each time. What a struck chord also passes for is its near twin and nothing else: of some 2,000 other chords it could have been taken for, about one in twenty lets it through, each time a chord that shares all but one key with it (C for C7, F for Fmaj7, Em7 for Cmaj7), which is meant so; for a chord with less than 0.6 of its keys in common it passed four times in 2,000, in the livelier room only. A tune and a broken chord are named key by key on such a piano as well;
- takes the chords of real pianos for what they are. Played by hand: all six chords of the grand, the C of the out-of-tune piano at home, and the six chords played one after the other, in their order. Of the upright in a room: every triad and seventh whose lowest key lies between G2 and B5, 41 of 41, twelve of them exactly where the app shows a chord and the others up to an octave and a half below and an octave above; further down 34 of 41; and at the very top of the keyboard, where the listener does not look, 11 of 20. Of the 100 chords played by hand anywhere on the keyboard: 93, the seven misses being mostly clips from the ends of the keyboard or with the chord before still ringing in them;
- does not take a single key for a chord, though its harmonics spell one (the G above middle C sounds G, D and B): none of the 17 keys from middle C to the E an octave above it, where the app's chords lie. In the simulated room one of them does pass (the E for E minor), and so do a key or two outside that range. A chord with one key struck four times as hard as the others is still heard as the chord;
- played into the app through the microphone input of the browser, the recorded piano gets through a phrase of whole chords and a phrase of broken chords without a slip, through the chord check key by key and as a whole, and through a change counted in its minute.
- on the real voices hears 98 % of the singing, and 96 % of its readings lie within 50 cents of the written-down pitch (94 % in the same octave); of 630 notes held for 0.3 s or more it judges 620 right by the middle of what was heard (as with the click), and takes 624 as held while it waits, a semitone above or below the note only 17 times in 1,260. The first rule for a held note, every reading within 50 cents for a quarter of a second, let only 551 of the 630 through: a voice with vibrato swings round its note by half a semitone and more. Now the middle of the last quarter second counts, with half the readings inside the band. A pitch is named about 0.05 s after it was sung; the click allows for that.

The browser check also plays the recorded guitar and the recorded piano into the app through the microphone input.

Testing against the recordings changed the listener a good deal. The first version, tuned on synthesized sounds only, got 45 of the 110 chord changes right, called a third of the clean nylon-string notes muted and missed low notes among ringing strings. The chords of real pianos changed it once more: a chord with one loud key had been taken for that key alone and not heard as a chord at all, and a chord struck an octave or two below the place the app shows it in had been confused with its relatives, because low down more of its harmonics lie where the listener looks. Both are taken care of, without a change to what is heard in the app's own place.

**What that does not cover.**

- **Your instrument, your microphone, your room.** The single notes are four guitars and one piano, close up and without a room; only the six strummed pieces have a real room in them. To see what a room does to single notes, the tests also play them through a simulated living room (reverberation of half a second, nearly as loud as the note itself, little bass): notes, chord changes and clean strings come out the same, but the room keeps a dead string sounding. In a lesson a dead string then passes as the note it was meant to be in 43 of 108 tries, against 16 without the room; in the string check 4 of the 108 pass as clean, and none once the app has learned from clean notes how long the guitar rings. That is a simulation. A laptop microphone a metre away in your living room is the real test, and the first minutes with your own instrument will show it. When something is not heard right, **Settings → When something is not heard right → Record** saves up to 30 seconds of exactly what the app heard as a sound file in your downloads folder, and `npm run hear -- <that file>` prints what the listener makes of it, moment by moment: what was struck, which note or chord it took it for and how sure it was, how long a string rang and how much it buzzed against what the settings ask for. That turns "it does not hear my G string" into numbers a threshold can be set by.
- **Buzzing strings.** There are no recordings of fret buzz to test against; that part is tested with synthesized sounds only. If clean strings are called buzzing, or buzzing ones pass, move **Settings → Buzz tolerance**.
- **Half-muted strings.** A dead string is recognised. A string that is only dulled still rings a little: of the steel-string notes played with the hand on the strings, the string check calls 38 of 90 muted at first, 50 once it has learned from clean notes how long the guitar rings, and 60 with **Settings → How long a string must ring** turned up to 0.2. None of the 108 clean notes is called muted at any of these.
- **Strummed chords are matched, not transcribed.** The app decides which of the chords it is waiting for you played; it cannot tell whether every string in a strum was clean. That is what the chord check is for.
- **The click is a high tick on purpose.** It sits above the range the listener analyses, so it does not matter that the microphone hears it from the speakers. If you cannot hear it well, turn it up, or use headphones and switch **Settings → Metronome sound** to the low click.
- **Timing with the click** depends on how long sound takes from the speakers to the microphone and into the app. **Settings → Timing with the click → Measure now** plays six clicks and times them; without that the app starts from what the browser reports and corrects itself while you play along. Bluetooth speakers add a lot of delay: wired is better.
- **Singing.** The real voices sing their own songs, not the app's, and were recorded close up; how your voice, microphone and room come through is yours to find out. Words are not judged, only pitch and timing; a song is sung on “la”. The app cannot tell how a note is produced (support, strain, registers): the handbook says what to watch for, and a voice that gets hoarse should rest. Its own model voice is heard through the microphone too; while it sings, the app does not listen.
- Notes below the low D of a guitar and fast runs of more than about five notes a second are outside what it was built for. On the piano it follows one hand: single keys, or a chord struck as a whole.
- **Chords on the piano are matched, like strums.** The app decides which of the chords it is waiting for was struck; it cannot tell for sure whether every key of it went down. That is what the chord check, key by key, is for. The piano's chords were tested with the single keys of one recorded grand mixed together (through a simulated room, detuned key by key, struck unevenly, with the pedal held), with an upright in a room whose keys the instrument moves itself, and with a few dozen chords that people played by hand on three other pianos. That is not your piano, your hand and your room: how an old upright in a living room is heard, only playing it will show. If a chord is not taken, **Settings → Record** saves what the app heard, and `npm run hear -- <file> instrument=piano chords=C,F,G` says what the listener made of it. The app's own practice piano can only be heard striking the same chord again about twice a second, which is why no piece asks for it faster.

## How the songs were checked

The tunes and the chords, too, were checked against something outside the app. The tunes that had been written from memory were compared note for note with published notation (the scores in Wikipedia's articles, Mutopia and the ABC collections); in several a rhythm was corrected (a note held too short, an upbeat missing, bars one note off), and songs that had only their first strain were completed from the sources. A song that begins with an upbeat has its click on the bar line.

The chord charts of the public songbook were compared with published tune books and hymnals (the ABC tune books on abcnotation.com, the songbooks of Wikibooks, hymnary.org and collections of folk songs with chords), as scale degrees, so that a chart in another key still compares. Of the 43 traditional songs, spirituals, carols and hymns added for the published version, 9 agreed as they stood and 33 were corrected: most often a chord one bar early or late, a form that was too short or too long, a dominant where the tune holds the tonic. One song for which no chart tied to the bars could be found was left out. Every song's public-domain status was checked as well (composer and lyricist, first publication): two whose status is unclear in one country were left out of the published book. The tempi are from experience, not from sources.

## What it is built on

Before the learning design was built, the well-known guitar, piano and singing apps and courses were compared with each other and with research on practice and on singing. The learning path, the song levels, the tips for chord changes and the way the voice is measured come from that comparison. From it as well: **Hear it** (H) in a lesson plays the line as a model, a little slower than full speed, before you try it; what the microphone picks up of that is not counted as your playing. A bar that slipped in a lesson is played once more, alone, before the lesson moves on. A chord that already rings (level 2 or more) is asked for from memory in the chord check: its picture stays hidden until you press S or a string does not ring. And the learning path says on how many different days something was measured clean ("on 3 days"), because one good day may be luck.
